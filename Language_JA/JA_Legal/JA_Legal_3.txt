/list common japanese legal terms

Here is a list of common Japanese legal terms related to defamation, civil wrongs, and criminal offenses, along with their English equivalents and breakdown.

Defamation and Reputation
    • 名誉毀損 (meiyo kison): Defamation of character (covers both written libel and spoken slander).
	
    • 侮辱罪 (bujokuzai): Insult crime (criminal offense for publicly demeaning someone without needing to prove a factual claim).
	
    • 信用毀損 (shinyō kison): Damage to credit or financial reputation (spreading false rumors to harm a person's or business's economic trust).
	
    • プライバシー侵害 (puraibashī shingai): Infringement of privacy.
	
    • 肖像権侵害 (shōzōken shingai): Violation of portrait rights (unauthorized use or publication of someone’s image or likeness). 
	
Civil Wrongs and Liability
    • 不法行為 (fuhō kōi): Tort (an illegal act or civil wrong that causes harm to another person).
	
    • 損害賠償 (songai baishō): Damages / Compensation for damages.
	
    • 慰謝料 (isharyō): Solatium / Consolation money (compensation specifically for mental distress or emotional suffering).
	
    • 差止請求 (sashitome seikyū): Injunction request (demanding someone stop a harmful action, such as deleting a defamatory post).
	
    • 発信者情報開示請求 (hasshinsha jōhō kaiji seikyū): Disclosure request for sender information (used to identify anonymous online trolls or slanderers).

Criminal Law Basics
    • 告訴 (kokusu): Criminal complaint (filed by a victim to request the police prosecute a criminal offender).
	
    • 告発 (kokuhatsu): Accusation / Denunciation (filed by a third party, not the victim, to report a crime).
	
    • 被害届 (higaitodoke): Damage report / Incident report (informing the police that a crime occurred, without an explicit request for prosecution).
	
    • 起訴 (kiso): Indictment / Prosecution.
	
    • 有罪 (yūzai): Guilty verdict.

/dive deeper into these legal  terms

To truly understand how Japan addresses slander, libel, and harassment, you have to look at the sharp distinction Japanese law makes between alleging facts (Meiyo Kison) versus hurling pure insults (Bujokuzai), alongside the specific legal avenues victims use to unmask anonymous internet users.

1. Defamation vs. Insults: The Crucial Difference
In Japanese criminal law, defamation and insults are handled under entirely separate articles of the Penal Code. The core difference rests on whether a specific "fact" was alleged.

名誉毀損 (Meiyo Kison) — Defamation of Character
    • The Legal Definition: Regulated under Article 230-1 of the Penal Code. It occurs when someone publicly defames another person by alleging specific facts, regardless of whether those facts are true or false.
    • Example: Posting online, "Company Executive X is embezzling money from the charity fund." Because it alleges a specific fact, it falls under Meiyo Kison.
	
    • Criminal Penalties: Imprisonment (with or without work) for up to 3 years, or a fine of up to ¥500,000.
	
    • The "Public Interest" Defense: Under Article 230-2, a defendant can avoid punishment if they prove three things: the statement concerns matters of public interest, was made solely for the public benefit, and the alleged facts are completely true.

侮辱罪 (Bujokuzai) — Insult Crime
    • The Legal Definition: Regulated under Article 231. This applies when someone publicly demeans or insults another person without alleging specific facts.
    • Example: Posting online, "You are ugly, stupid, and a total piece of trash." No specific factual claim is being made, but the target's abstract social standing is demeaned.
	
    • Criminal Penalties: Following high-profile cyberbullying tragedies (such as the passing of reality TV star Hana Kimura), Japan heavily toughened these penalties. It is now punishable by up to 1 year in prison or a fine of up to ¥300,000. [1, 2]

2. Civil Remedies: Getting Paid for Damages

If a victim prefers to sue for financial compensation rather than push for jail time, they look to the Civil Code under standard tort law (Fuhō Kōi).

    • 損害賠償 (Songai Baishō): General compensation for damages. If a restaurant loses customers because of a fake 1-star review campaign, they sue for the literal revenue lost due to the damage to their business reputation (Shinyō Kison).
	
    • 慰謝料 (Isharyō): Emotional distress damages or "solatium". This is the financial compensation paid strictly for the mental pain and suffering caused by the slander. 

3. The Digital Battlefield: Unmasking Internet Trolls
Because most modern slander happens anonymously online, a victim cannot file a civil lawsuit or a criminal complaint without knowing who the perpetrator is.

    • 発信者情報開示請求 (Hasshinsha Jōhō Kaiji Seikyū): This translates to a Disclosure Request for Sender Information.
	
    • The Simplified One-Step Process: Historically, victims had to go through a grueling, multi-step court process—first suing the social media platform (like X or Instagram) to get an IP address, and then suing the Internet Service Provider (like SoftBank or Docomo) to link that IP to a real name and home address.
	
    • How it works now: Japan uses a streamlined, single-step court process called a "non-contentious procedure". A victim can apply for a single integrated court order. If approved, the court simultaneously forces the platform and the service provider to hand over the troll's name, physical address, phone number, and email. Once this information is secured, the victim can formally serve them with a Songai Baishō lawsuit or head to the police station with a criminal Kokusu (complaint)
